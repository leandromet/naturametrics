How to read the landscape around a point in the United States, one layer at a time

One click on the map, and Naturametrics draws five circles around it and reads forty-one years of land cover, of disturbance, of fire severity and of tree canopy inside each. This walkthrough opens a real point, Paradise, California, step by step, with where each number comes from and where it stops.

1

It all starts from a point

Naturametrics does not need a property line. A click, an address, a ZIP code or a coordinate is enough: the analysis is built around the point, so it works the same on a ranch, a park, a national forest or a backyard.

In the app: the Search box on the USA page, a click on the map, or a direct link with /usa?lat=…&lon=….

2

Five circles around the point

Every analysis runs five times, in circles of 0.5, 1, 2, 5 and 10 km. The small ones say what is at the point; the large ones, what the landscape around it is like. Each circle contains the smaller ones, so the 10 km circle is the whole picture and the 0.5 km one is the close-up.

In the app: the Radius selector above the charts, and the circles on the map.

3

Forty-one land-cover maps in one circle

The USGS Annual National Land Cover Database classifies every 30 m pixel of the lower 48 each year, from 1985 to 2025. Each column is one year in the 5 km circle, and each colour a class: forest, shrub, grassland, crops, wetland, water and four levels of development.

In the app: the Land-cover history chart, and the Annual NLCD layer with its year selector.

4

Near and far tell different stories

The same series as the share of each circle that is natural vegetation: forest, wetland, grassland and shrub. When the small circles and the large ones move together, the change is regional; when they part, the point is doing something its surroundings are not.

In the app: the Land-cover history chart with the % button, changing radius.

5

The point's own pixel, no averaging

Before any circle, one pixel: the 30 m square under the click, year by year. It is the sharpest reading and the most fragile, because a single misclassified year shows up as a change.

In the app: the clicked pixel's class, year by year, is a sheet of the point spreadsheet (Download data).

6

What changed, and when

The Forest Service's Landscape Change Monitoring System reads the Landsat record year by year and labels what it sees: wildfire, tree removal, insects and drought, wind, and the regrowth that follows. Each column is the area it flags in the 5 km circle that year.

In the app: the Disturbance history chart and the Last disturbance (LCMS) layer.

7

How hard the fire burned

MTBS maps the perimeter and the burn severity of the large fires: low, moderate or high. It is a separate mapping, made from before-and-after images of each fire, so it dates the fire where LCMS dates its effect. It only maps fires of at least 1,000 acres in the west (500 in the east): smaller ones are not here.

In the app: the Fire (MTBS) chart and the Fire severity layer.

8

How long since the forest was disturbed

The United States has no measured map of stand age, so this is its nearest honest equivalent: for each pixel that is tree cover today, the years since the last fire, cutting or insect outbreak LCMS detected. The clock starts in 1985, so the last bar means "not disturbed since 1985", and says nothing about how old those trees are.

In the app: the Time since disturbance chart.

9

How much canopy there is

The Forest Service's Tree Canopy Cover estimates, for each 30 m pixel and year, the share covered by tree crowns. The mean over a circle is the simplest summary of a landscape's tree cover, and it tracks both loss and regrowth from 1985 on.

In the app: the Tree canopy cover chart and layer.

10

A second opinion on the loss

Hansen/GLAD is a different, global product: it flags, by year since 2001, the pixels that lost their tree cover. Where it and LCMS agree, the story is firmer. Hansen books a pixel in the year the loss is first seen, so a late-year fire may fall partly in the next.

In the app: the Forest change chart, the Loss and gain layer, and the Forest threshold control.

11

Everything on screen, in one spreadsheet

Each analysis keeps its source, scale and time of calculation, and the export carries them with the numbers: a reading of a landscape is only worth what it takes to reproduce it.

In the app: the Download data button: an ODS spreadsheet with one sheet per table.

What Naturametrics does not say

  • It does not tell you why the land changed. LCMS and MTBS label causes from the pattern in the imagery; a fire, a salvage logging and a clearing can look alike to a satellite. The cause is for the forest record or a visit.
  • It does not date a stand. "Time since disturbance" starts counting in 1985; a stand not disturbed since then is open-ended, not forty-one years old.
  • It does not map every fire. MTBS covers fires of at least 1,000 acres in the west and 500 in the east; LCMS sees smaller disturbances, with more noise.
  • It does not count carbon. Canopy and disturbance describe the landscape; a carbon figure would need an inventory and growth curves the tool does not have.
  • It cannot see below 30 m. A narrow riparian strip or a backyard grove is a few pixels at most.

Notes

  1. The three UmaTerra tools differ in who draws the boundary they analyse: the state (protected and Indigenous lands), the landholder (a cadastre) or the analyst (a point on the map). Naturametrics is the analyst's tool: it draws no boundary at all, only circles of fixed size, so two points anywhere are compared on equal terms. Biondo et al., Registry, coordinate, cadastre (manuscript in preparation, 2026).
  2. LCMS assigns a change to the year in which the Landsat record first shows it, which for a fire late in the year is the following one. That is why the Camp Fire of November 2018 shows as 2019 here and as 2018 in MTBS, and the two columns should be read together. USFS GTAC, Landscape Change Monitoring System, v2025-11.
  3. Brazil's page derives vegetation age the same way, from a count of years since a pixel was last cleared, and for the same reason reports the oldest class as open-ended. Canada's page can show a measured stand age because Canada publishes one; the United States does not. Biondo et al., Registry, coordinate, cadastre (manuscript in preparation, 2026).
